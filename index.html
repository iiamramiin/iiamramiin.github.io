I design digital hardware, and lately I study how AI can help design it.

I'm a Senior FPGA Design Engineer at Cisco, with more than ten years of experience turning specs into working hardware. I care about hardware that is correct, observable, and reliable, and about the tools that help engineers get there faster.

What I'm working on now

FPGAs for networking hardware

At Cisco I own several FPGA and CPLD designs for networking and data-center products, from architecture and RTL through verification, timing closure, and lab bring-up. I also build reusable SystemVerilog IPs and the flows that take designs from code to hardware.

Making hardware easier to debug

I'm interested in hardware that explains itself when something goes wrong. That includes debug and observability features built into the design, and machine learning that spots faults early from hardware telemetry.

Testing how well models write RTL

On my own time I build rtlgym, an environment where language models write SystemVerilog and get graded the way a hardware team would grade it.

Interests

AI for chip design. Language models can write Verilog that compiles and passes a few tests, yet is still wrong. I'm interested in how we measure that gap honestly. That means hidden tests, formal equivalence, synthesis results, and graders that can't be gamed. I'm also interested in how tool feedback and reinforcement learning help close the gap.

Verification and correctness. Most of the risk in hardware sits in what nobody tested. I like methods that cover more than the testbench author imagined: assertions, coverage, and formal checks against a reference.

Reliable hardware. My Ph.D. was on soft errors in nanoscale CMOS, and I still care about how hardware fails, and how to detect it, tolerate it, and debug it in the field.

Hardware for machine learning. As a researcher I worked on in-memory computing, ferroelectric embedded memory, and analog hardware for neural networks. I've also run fixed-point neural-network models on FPGAs in real products.

rtlgym

An open-source environment for evaluating language models that write hardware, built so it can also serve as a reinforcement-learning environment. A model gets a spec, writes a SystemVerilog design, and can revise it after seeing the tools' feedback. The 12 tasks range from an ALU and a FIFO to a 5-port network-on-chip router and an AXI4-Lite slave.

Each attempt is compiled first, then checked four ways in parallel, usually in one to two seconds. A design is accepted only if it passes all hidden tests, is formally equivalent to the reference, and synthesizes without latches. The grader also rejects common reward hacks, like ending the simulation early or reading files.

Background

I started out building onboard computers for a student satellite at Sharif University of Technology (SharifSat), including a radiation-hardened LEON3 processor. I earned my Ph.D. in Electrical Engineering there as well. I then joined the faculty of Shahid Beheshti University, where I directed the High-Performance Computing Lab, and later did research at the University of Notre Dame on emerging-device circuits for memory, security, and machine learning. In 2021 I moved to industry, first building FPGA platforms to validate RF transceiver ASICs at Jariet Technologies. Since 2024 I have been at Cisco, designing the FPGAs and CPLDs inside its networking and data-center products. The role brings together everything that came before: RTL design, verification, and making hardware work reliably once it ships.

Along the way I published 60+ papers (1,550+ citations) and taught 14 courses including ASIC/FPGA design.
